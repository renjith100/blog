Why one great chip can't catch NVIDIA

Imagine a rival wakes up tomorrow with a chip every bit as fast as NVIDIA's best. It still wouldn't be enough. What makes NVIDIA hard to catch was never only in the silicon. It's four things stacked on top of each other, built over 33 years, one bet at a time. 0:00

Decision 1 · 1993–1997

Bet on curves, lose, start over

NVIDIA was founded in April 1993 by Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem, famously over meals at a Denny's in San Jose. Their first chip, the NV1 (1995), drew 3D shapes with curved surfaces (quadratic patches). It was elegant: a few curves describe a smooth ball.

Then Microsoft shipped Direct3D, the standard every Windows game would use, and it was built on triangles. A triangle is dumb, but it's the simplest shape there is, so hardware can draw billions of them, and anything can be built from enough of them. The NV1 bet was dead. 0:25

NVIDIA was also building a curves chip for Sega's next console. Jensen told Sega's president that the design was wrong and that Sega should go elsewhere, and asked Sega to pay the contract anyway so NVIDIA could survive. Sega invested $5 million. That money kept the lights on.

He laid off about half of the roughly 100 staff and started again, on triangles. The new chip, the RIVA 128, shipped in August 1997 when the company had about one month of payroll left. It sold around a million units in four months. 0:37

Decision 2 · 1997–2000

Anyone can draw triangles, so win on speed

Triangles were a standard, so every rival could draw them. Jensen's answer was to be faster at shipping, not just faster in silicon. Three moves made that possible: 0:43

  • No factory. NVIDIA designs chips and lets TSMC in Taiwan build them. Jensen wrote to TSMC's founder Morris Chang in 1997, and Chang phoned him back. Within a few years NVIDIA was one of TSMC's biggest customers. Not owning a fab meant NVIDIA's money went into design.
  • Test the chip before it exists. For the RIVA 128, NVIDIA spent a big slice of its remaining cash on an emulator, a machine that runs the chip's design in software. That let them fix bugs before a real chip was made, instead of waiting months for each test chip from the factory.
  • Three teams in overlapping waves. A chip still takes a long time to design. But with three teams staggered, one team finishes every six months. Rivals shipped a new chip every year or two.

Decision 3 · 1999

Name it the GPU: a genius vs an army

In 1999, the year NVIDIA went public, it launched the GeForce 256 and called it "the world's first GPU", a graphics processing unit. The chip moved more of the 3D work off the main processor. The name was also marketing, and it worked: NVIDIA got to define the category it was selling into. That mix of real engineering and loud naming became a habit. 0:57

Why does a picture need a different kind of chip? A 1080p screen holds about 2 million pixels (1920 × 1080 = 2,073,600). Repaint it 60 times a second and that's about 124 million pixel colours every second, each one needing its own small calculation (light, shadow, texture). 1:03

The key: each pixel's maths doesn't depend on its neighbour's. A CPU is a genius with a few brilliant cores that solve one hard thing after another. A GPU is an army of thousands of simple workers doing the same small job on different pixels at the same time.

Decision 4 · 2006

CUDA: a road built before there were cars

Scientists noticed that same army could crunch physics, finance, medical scans: any maths that splits into many independent pieces. But the army only spoke graphics. To use it you had to disguise your numbers as the colours of a picture and your calculation as "shading" it. A Stanford PhD student, Ian Buck, built a research language (Brook) to hide that trick. NVIDIA hired him. 1:16

In November 2006, with the GeForce 8800, NVIDIA launched CUDA: a way to program the GPU in ordinary C. And Jensen made a costly call. CUDA would go into every GeForce card, even the cheap gaming ones, so every gamer's PC became a machine CUDA could run on. Every chip carried extra circuitry and extra cost that gamers didn't pay for.

For years almost nobody used it. Then the 2008 crash hit, along with a costly batch of faulty laptop chips. NVIDIA's shares fell more than 75%, and the whole company was worth only around $4 billion at the bottom. Wall Street wanted the CUDA spending cut. Jensen kept paying for the road. 1:27

Decision 5 · 2012–2016

AI finds its engine, and NVIDIA bets the company on it






ImageNet was the big yearly contest for computer vision: 1.2 million labelled photos, 1,000 categories, and each program gets five guesses per photo. In 2012 a team from the University of Toronto, professor Geoffrey Hinton and his students Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever, entered a neural network now called AlexNet.


It didn't follow hand-written rules about edges and shapes. It learned to see by adjusting about 60 million numbers (its parameters) over and over against the photos. It was trained on two GeForce GTX 580 gaming cards in Krizhevsky's bedroom, over five to six days, using CUDA.


The runner-up missed on more than 1 photo in 4. AlexNet missed on fewer than 1 in 6. In a field that usually moved by a point or two a year, that was a cliff. 1:35

Bet the company


Jensen saw it early. Deep learning was exactly the army's kind of work: huge piles of simple multiply-and-add maths, all independent. Around 2013–2014 he told the company NVIDIA was now an AI computing company, and pointed every team at it: libraries like cuDNN (2014) that made neural nets fast on GPUs, and chips designed for AI rather than games.


In April 2016 NVIDIA announced the DGX-1, a box of eight GPUs sold as an "AI supercomputer" for $129,000. In August 2016 Jensen hand-delivered the very first one to a six-month-old non-profit lab in San Francisco, OpenAI, and signed it. 1:55


It was a gift with a strategy. In 2016 NVIDIA was still seen as a gaming company, worth about $35 billion. Putting its hardware in the hands of the most visible AI lab made the point publicly, seeded models built natively on CUDA, and gave NVIDIA's engineers a direct view of what frontier AI training needed next. Six years later, OpenAI's ChatGPT ran on NVIDIA GPUs.

The moat · software

Change the chip, and the work starts over






Twenty years after launch, NVIDIA says more than 11 million developers build on CUDA. But the real moat isn't CUDA itself. It's everything piled on top of it. 2:01


Almost nobody writes raw CUDA. AI researchers write in PyTorch. PyTorch calls NVIDIA's libraries: cuDNN for neural-net layers, cuBLAS for matrix maths, NCCL for GPUs talking to each other, TensorRT for serving models fast. Those were hand-tuned by NVIDIA engineers, chip generation after chip generation, for two decades. On top sit thousands of research papers, tutorials, Stack Overflow answers, hiring pipelines, and the habits of every ML engineer alive.

Decision 6, part 1 · 2019

When one machine became thousands, the cables became the computer






AlexNet fit on two cards. Today's frontier models are trained on tens of thousands of GPUs at once. Each GPU works on its slice, then all of them must share their results before the next step can start. If the links between them are slow, thousands of very expensive chips sit idle, waiting.


So in March 2019 NVIDIA agreed to buy Mellanox, an Israeli networking company, for $6.9 billion (it closed in April 2020). Mellanox made InfiniBand, the fast, low-delay network used in supercomputers. Together with NVIDIA's own NVLink (a direct GPU-to-GPU link) and NVSwitch, NVIDIA could now sell the network as well as the chips, and tune the two together. 2:12

Decision 6, part 2 · 2024 →

Blackwell: selling a rack instead of a chip






Announced in March 2024, Blackwell hit a wall that every chip hits: a chip factory can only print a die up to a certain size (the "reticle limit"). So NVIDIA made one GPU out of two of the largest possible dies, joined by a link so fast (10 TB/s) that software sees them as a single chip. Together: 208 billion transistors.


It also does maths in FP4, 4-bit numbers. Neural nets tolerate rough numbers well, and halving the bits roughly doubles how much maths fits through the chip. Its "Transformer Engine" picks the precision layer by layer.


But the real product isn't the chip. It's the GB200 NVL72: one rack holding 72 Blackwell GPUs and 36 of NVIDIA's own Grace CPUs, all wired by NVLink so they behave like one giant GPU. Tap the parts.

The whole game

To catch NVIDIA, rebuild four things at once


Chips, software, connections and pace. Each one is hard. Together they reinforce each other: the software makes the chips easy to buy, the connections make thousands of chips work as one, and the yearly pace means a rival that catches up has caught up with last year's NVIDIA. Why China can't just buy, or build, its way in


Since 2022 the US has kept NVIDIA's most advanced AI chips out of China. 0:10 NVIDIA kept designing cut-down chips under each new limit, and the limits kept moving. Chinese labs have kept building strong models anyway, with older chips, smuggled chips and very clever software. But the full stack (fast chips made in volume, mature software, rack-scale networking, and a yearly pace) is exactly the thing a ban makes hardest to assemble.


Huawei's answer shows the four-layer problem: its CloudMatrix 384 links 384 Ascend chips with optical cables to make up in numbers and power what each chip lacks, and its CANN software is being built to replace CUDA, all while China's chipmakers work without the most advanced lithography machines.




    

    

    



    
The playbook

    How Jensen runs the company
    

    Skill and showmanship, in roughly equal measure, and the showmanship only works because the products ship.
    

    

    "30 days from going out of business"
    He said it for years, long after NVIDIA was rich. It started as the literal truth in 1997 and became a culture of urgency.
    

    Build for markets that don't exist yet
    He calls them "zero-billion-dollar markets". CUDA in 2006 and AI in 2012 had no customers when he started paying for them.
    

    Name the category
    "GPU" in 1999. "Accelerated computing". "AI factory". "Sovereign AI". Whoever names the category gets to define what winning in it looks like.
    

    Sell the whole system
    First the chip, then the card, then the DGX box, then the rack, then the blueprint for the whole data centre. Each step makes NVIDIA harder to swap out piece by piece.
    

    Flat and fast
    Around 60 people reporting straight to him, few one-on-ones, and staff emailing their "top five things" so news reaches him without layers of managers.
    

    Honest when it costs
    He told Sega its NV2 design was wrong while NVIDIA was nearly broke. Sega paid anyway, and the company survived on that trust.
    

    Hype, then ship
    The keynotes are pure theatre (the leather jacket, "the more you buy, the more you save"). But the roadmap he shows on stage usually arrives on time, which is why customers plan around it.
    

    Seed the future
    Hand-delivering the first DGX-1 to OpenAI. Free GPUs to university labs. Get the people who'll define the next decade building on your platform first.
